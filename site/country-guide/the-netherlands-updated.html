Payroll & Employment Guide: Netherlands

This guide covers what employers need to know before hiring in the Netherlands: employment contract rules, working-hour limits, notice periods, payroll tax bands, social security contributions, statutory leave entitlements, and work-permit requirements for non-EU hires. The Netherlands has a well-regulated labour market with strong employee protections and mandatory pension contributions for temporary or agency-employed staff. For a compliant route into the Dutch market without setting up a local entity, see our EOR Services in the Netherlands page, or compare EOR vs Global Payroll vs Recognised Sponsor to find the right structure for your hiring plan.

Hiring in the Netherlands

Dutch employment law is elaborate and relatively complex. It is divided into individual and collective law and is closely related to social security law. Key points:

  • Employment law is not consolidated into a single code.
  • Employees have a strong legal position.
  • Preventive dismissal assessment applies.
  • A relatively long period of salary payment during illness.
  • The Balanced Labour Market Act governs flexible and permanent contracts.

The Netherlands is a North-western European country of approximately 18 million inhabitants, known for its tradition of liberal social policies and high quality of life. Amsterdam is the country's most populous city and nominal capital. Dutch society is egalitarian and modern.

Employment Contract

An employment contract may be concluded orally or in writing. However, the employer must inform the employee in writing on key conditions, including:

  • Name and residence of the parties; place of work.
  • Position and job description; hiring date.
  • Duration (for fixed-term contracts); annual leave rights.
  • Notice periods; salary and payment intervals.
  • Customary working hours per day or week.
  • Pension rights (if applicable); applicable Collective Labour Agreement (CLA).

Contract Terms:

Since 1 January 2020, a temporary contract automatically converts to a permanent contract if an employee has received more than 3 successive temporary contracts, or has had temporary contracts with the same employer spanning more than 3 years (unless a CLA provides otherwise).

A fixed-term contract converts to open-ended if:

  1. A chain of temporary contracts covers 36 months or more, or
  2. A chain of three fixed-term contracts is continued with no more than 6 months between them.

One month before the end of a fixed-term contract of 6 months or longer, the employer must notify the employee whether the contract will be extended.

Probation Period:

  • Must be agreed in writing.
  • Not permitted for contracts of 6 months or less.
  • Maximum 1 month for fixed-term contracts between 6 months and 2 years.
  • Maximum 2 months for contracts longer than 2 years or open-ended contracts.
  • Either party may terminate immediately during probation, without notice or reasons.

Working Hours:

The average working week is 5 days with 36–40 hours. Statutory limits:

  • Maximum 12 hours per shift; maximum 60 hours per week.
  • Average of 55 hours per week over any 4-week period.
  • Average of 48 hours per week over any 16-week period.

Rest requirements:

  • 11 consecutive hours of rest after a working day (may be shortened to 8 hours once per 7-day period).
  • 36 consecutive hours of rest after a 5-day work week.
  • Alternatively, 72 consecutive hours of rest in any 14-day period (may be split into two periods of at least 32 hours).

Breaks:

  • Over 5.5 hours worked: at least 30 minutes (may be split into two 15-minute breaks).
  • Over 10 hours worked: at least 45 minutes (may be split, each part minimum 15 minutes).

Overtime:
There is no specific Dutch legislation mandating overtime pay. Compensation for overtime should follow from the employment contract or the applicable CLA. If no stipulations are made, Courts are likely to rule that a good employer should compensate the employee for overtime.

Notice Periods:

The notice period for employees is normally 1 month. By written agreement, this may be shortened or extended up to a maximum of 6 months; in such cases, the employer's notice period must be twice that of the employee.

Termination of Employment:

An open-ended employment contract can be terminated in the following ways:

  • Employer submits a termination request to the Dutch Labour Authorities (UWV) and gives notice after UWV permission.
  • Employee consents after the employer gives notice (no UWV permission required).
  • Court proceedings — for cases including underperformance, culpable acts, or disruptive absence.
  • Mutual consent — no mandatory notice period; parties may agree on a severance package.
  • Dismissal for urgent reason (serious misconduct) — must be communicated immediately, contract terminated without notice.
  • Notice given by the employee, observing the applicable notice period.

Applications for redundancy on economic grounds or long-term incapacity must go to the UWV. All other dismissals must be referred to the district court.

Collective Dismissal:
Applies when an employer dismisses 20 or more employees within 3 months within one UWV work area. The employer must notify the UWV and relevant trade unions, and observe a one-month waiting period (waived if trade unions confirm consultation and agreement).

Redundancy / Severance Pay:
Employees are entitled to a transition payment (transitievergoeding) from the first day of employment. The payment equals one-third of monthly salary per calendar year of service, capped at €102,000 gross, or one annual salary if higher. The transition payment is not owed if the employee terminates voluntarily (unless caused by serious employer misconduct). In cases of cumulative dismissal, the Court may grant an additional severance of up to half the transition payment.

Post-Termination Restraints / Restrictive Covenants:

  • Non-compete clauses: Must be agreed in writing and limited to what is reasonably necessary. Duration of one year is typically considered reasonable. Including a non-compete in a fixed-term contract is prohibited unless the employer can substantiate a substantial business interest in the contract itself. The employer can enforce via Court and claim damages or a penalty clause.
  • Non-solicitation clauses: May restrict the employee from soliciting the employer's customers or colleagues during or after employment. Enforceable via Court, typically with a penalty clause.

Employee Benefits

The Netherlands operates two types of compulsory insurance schemes. Employers pay contributions on behalf of employees to the Dutch Tax and Customs Administration (Belastingdienst) as part of the payroll tax. The Employee Insurance Agency (UWV) manages benefit payments.

Mandatory Benefits:

  • National Insurance Schemes (compulsory for all who live or work in the Netherlands):
    • General Child Benefit Act (AKW)
    • National Survivor Benefits Act (ANW)
    • General Old Age Pensions Act (AOW)
    • Long-term Care Act (WLZ)
  • Employee Insurance Schemes (compulsory for all employees):
    • Unemployment Insurance Act (WW)
    • Work and Income (Capacity for Work) Act (WIA)
    • Sickness Benefits Act (ZW)
    • Invalidity Insurance Act (WAO) – only for employees already receiving WAO before 1 January 2006
  • Holiday allowance: A mandatory holiday allowance of 8% of gross annual salary is paid, typically in May.
  • Bonus and 13th month pay: There are no mandatory bonus or commission payment requirements. Whether bonuses are customary depends on the industry and role. If bonuses are granted, all employees must be treated equally and criteria must be objective. In the financial sector, variable remuneration cannot exceed 20% of fixed remuneration — stricter than the 100% EU cap.

Leave Policies:

  • Annual Leave: Statutory annual leave for a full-time 40-hour employee is 20 days per year. Statutory days expire 6 months after the end of the year in which they accrued. Non-statutory (CLA or contractual) leave days remain valid for up to 5 years. Statutory leave days cannot be paid out while the employee is still employed. Minimum holiday allowance: 8% of gross salary.
  • Sick Leave: Employers must continue paying wages for up to 104 weeks of illness. Year 1: at least 70% of wages (topped up to minimum wage if necessary; many employers pay 100%). Year 2: at least 70% of wages (no minimum-wage top-up obligation). Illness related to pregnancy, childbirth, or organ donation: 100% pay is required.
  • Emergency / Bereavement Leave: Emergency leave is granted for unforeseen personal circumstances (e.g. sudden family illness or bereavement). Must always be granted on reasonable request; salary continues in full. Short-term care leave (care for ill parent, child or partner): up to 2x weekly working hours per year at 70% pay. Long-term care leave (life-threatening illness of close family): unpaid.
  • Special / Extraordinary Leave: Special leave is governed by the applicable CLA, company scheme, or employment contract. Typical entitlements:
    • Marriage or registered partnership — 2 days.
    • Death of spouse/partner, parent, or child — 4 days.
    • Funeral of grandparent, sibling, or in-law — 2 days.
    • Notice of intended marriage — 1 day.
    • Marriage of a family member — 1 day.
    • Wedding/employment anniversary milestones — 1 day.
    • Professional exam for accredited diploma — 1 day.
  • Maternity Leave: Pregnant employees are entitled to 4–6 weeks of pregnancy leave before the due date and at least 10 weeks of maternity leave after birth (total minimum 16 weeks). If less than 6 weeks are taken before birth, the remainder (up to 2 weeks) is added after. During maternity leave, the UWV pays 100% of the daily wage, capped at €304.25/day.
  • Partner / Paternity Leave: 1 week of partner leave immediately after birth, paid 100% by the employer. Partners may also take 5 weeks of extended partner leave within 6 months of birth, paid at 70% of the capped daily wage by the UWV.
  • Parental Leave: Employees are entitled to parental leave until the child turns 8 years old. Total entitlement: 26 times contracted weekly working hours. The first 9 weeks (if taken within 12 months of birth) are paid at 70% of daily wage by the UWV. Remaining leave is unpaid. Statutory holiday rights continue to accrue during the paid portion only.
  • Adoption / Foster Leave: 6 weeks of adoption or foster leave for both parents. The employer must be notified at least 3 weeks in advance. Leave may be spread over time; refusal is only possible in cases of serious operational impact.

Public Holidays (2026):
Employees in the Netherlands are entitled to paid public holidays. There are 10 public holidays per year (11 every 5 years). Whether employees are free on public holidays depends on the applicable CLA or individual contract. There is no substitute day if a holiday falls on a weekend.

Healthcare:

  • Dutch Social Health Contribution (ZVW): paid by the employer at 6.10%, monthly cap €6,617.42 (see Payroll › Taxes & Social Security).
  • Health and Safety in the Workplace: Employers must use the services of a working-conditions service (Arbodienst) that assists with risk assessments, advises on reintegration of sick employees, and supports sick employees. Employers must also inform employees of their right to address health and safety matters with the occupational health and safety service agency.
  • Group Healthcare Insurance: Employer-arranged collective health insurance at preferential rates (typical, non-statutory benefit).

Taxes in the Netherlands

In the Netherlands, worldwide income is taxed if you live abroad but have Dutch-source income; residents are taxed on worldwide income. Income is taxed according to payroll tax tables — the employer withholds tax and pays it to the Dutch Tax Authority (Belastingdienst).

Tax Allowances & Reliefs:

  • 30% Ruling: The 30% ruling is a tax advantage for highly skilled workers hired from abroad to work in the Netherlands. If granted, up to 30% of the employee's gross salary may be paid tax-free for a period of up to 5 years. The ruling is not automatic — the employer and employee must submit a joint request to the Dutch Tax Authority (Belastingdienst). See our full guide: The 30% Ruling — 2026 Thresholds for Skilled Migrants.
  • Work-Related Costs Scheme (WKR): All employers in the Netherlands must apply the Work-Related Costs Scheme (Werkkostenregeling – WKR). Under this scheme, any employer-provided benefit is generally treated as taxable salary unless it qualifies for an exemption. The two main categories are:
    • Discretionary Scope ('Vrije Ruimte') – a tax-free budget calculated as: 2.0% of the first €400,000 of the total taxable wage bill; 1.18% of the amount exceeding €400,000. Excess benefits allocated within the discretionary scope attract an 80% final levy on the excess amount.
    • Targeted Exemptions ('Gerichte Vrijstellingen') – fully tax-exempt, not counted against the discretionary scope. Examples: travel allowance (up to €0.23 per kilometre in 2026); education, courses, and professional training; work-related equipment such as laptops and mobile phones; temporary work-related meals and overnight accommodation.

Note: The information provided above is intended for general guidance only and should not be considered tax or legal advice. Always consult qualified tax advisors or payroll experts familiar with Dutch regulations for up-to-date requirements.

Payroll in the Netherlands

Base Salary: Salary is calculated monthly in arrears.

Minimum Wage (reference levels):

  • Age 21+ (full-time): approx. €14.40/hour (about €2,437.07 gross per month)
  • Age 20: €11.52/hour
  • Age 19: €8.64/hour
  • Age 18: €7.20/hour
  • Age 17: €5.69/hour
  • Age 16: €4.97/hour
  • Age 15: €4.32/hour

Payment Schedule:

  • Paid around the 25th of each month (or as specified in the CLA).
  • Net salary must be transferred to a bank account in the employee's name.
  • Payment to a third-party account is only permitted in the case of wage garnishment.
  • A 13th or 14th month payment is optional, at the employer's discretion, and commonly paid in December.

Taxes & Social Security:
Employers withhold employees' national insurance contributions from salary and also pay separate social security premiums. Social security has three main components: Social Security charges, Dutch Social Health Contribution (ZVW), and Pension.

Payslip Requirements:
Payslips must include: gross pay and its composition (basic wage, bonus, etc.), deductions for taxes and contributions, minimum holiday allowance, employer and employee names, the period covered, and contracted working hours.

Payroll Reporting:

  • Monthly: payroll tax returns covering wages, taxes, and contributions.
  • Quarterly: certain social security contributions may be reported quarterly.
  • Annual: a yearly earnings and withholding summary goes to employees by 31 January, plus a final reconciliation with the tax authorities.

Key Deadlines:

  • Salary payment: around the 25th of each month (or as specified in the CLA).
  • Taxes and contributions: monthly payroll filings.
  • Payroll declarations: monthly, quarterly, and annually as required.

Legal Regulations

Total Employment Cost:
Employer social security contributions: approximately 20–25% of gross salary.

Minimum Wage:
Reference minimum wage: €12.38 per hour (subject to periodic adjustment).

Probation Period:
Must be agreed in writing; not permitted for contracts of 6 months or less; maximum 1 month for fixed-term contracts between 6 months and 2 years; maximum 2 months for contracts longer than 2 years or open-ended contracts.

Timesheets:
Following the 2019 European Court of Justice ruling, employers must implement an objective, reliable, and accessible system to record each employee's daily working time. In the Netherlands, additional obligations apply under the WTTA law, requiring employment agencies to maintain transparent working-hours records.

Trade Unions / Collective Agreements:
The Social and Economic Council (SER) oversees proposed changes in employment law. Union membership is not compulsory; only around 16% of Dutch workers are unionised — a low rate by EU standards. Unions participate in politics through lobbying and the SER but have no official political-party affiliations. Transparent employer-employee relations mean the Netherlands experiences fewer strikes than most EU counterparts.

Work Permits & Visas for the Netherlands

Workers with Dutch, EEA, or Swiss nationality may work in the Netherlands without a work permit. Workers from outside the EEA and Switzerland generally require one of the following.

Types of Permits:

  • Employment Permit (TWV): for work periods of less than 3 months. Applied for by the employer only.
  • Single Permit (GVVA): combined residence and work permit for stays exceeding 3 months. The foreign national may apply directly.

An employer can only hire from outside the EEA/Switzerland if no suitable EEA/Swiss candidate can be found; the vacancy has been open for at least 5 weeks (or 3 months for hard-to-fill roles, as determined by the UWV); and the employer has demonstrably done everything possible to recruit locally.

BSN – Dutch Citizen Service Number:
All residents in the Netherlands must obtain a Burgerservicenummer (BSN) — the national identification number used for taxes, healthcare, housing, and social security. Registration in the BRP (Basisregistratie Personen) is required to obtain a BSN, which functions simultaneously as a social security number, national ID number, and tax number.

Most nationals from outside the EEA and Switzerland need Dutch work authorisation before starting work. For many roles, a TWV (employment permit) is used for work lasting under three months, while a GVVA combines residence and work permission for stays over three months. Highly skilled migrants follow a separate route and need an employer recognised by the IND as a sponsor, as well as meeting the applicable salary and other criteria. Employers with Dutch payroll obligations must withhold wage tax and applicable national insurance contributions. For employees below state pension age, 2026 combined rates are 35.75% up to €38,883, 37.56% from €38,883 to €78,426, and 49.50% above €78,426. Employer premiums include AWf (2.74% low / 7.74% high), Aof (6.27% / 7.63%), Wko (0.50%), variable Whk and the 6.10% Zvw employer levy. Actual liabilities depend on employee status and payroll classification; these are not one flat employer rate.

The cost depends on the reason for dismissal, salary, length of service, notice obligations and any settlement. Where an employer dismisses an employee or does not renew a fixed-term contract, a statutory transition payment is generally due from the first day of employment. It is calculated at one-third of gross monthly salary per year of service, pro-rated for part-years; an annual maximum applies and is indexed. A mutual termination agreement may instead involve negotiated compensation. Economic or long-term incapacity dismissals generally require UWV permission; other grounds may require court approval.

Employers in the Netherlands must generally continue paying a sick employee at least 70% of wages for up to 104 weeks (two years). During the first year, pay must be topped up to at least the statutory minimum wage if 70% falls below it; this top-up is not required in year two. A contract or applicable CAO may require higher sick pay. Employers must also follow occupational-health and reintegration steps with the employee. Failure to meet reintegration duties can lead UWV to require up to one additional year of wage payment.

Dutch employees are also generally entitled to at least 8% holiday allowance (vakantiegeld) on eligible gross wages, commonly paid once a year in May or June. A contract or applicable Collective Labour Agreement (CAO) may provide more leave or different payment arrangements. Public holidays are not automatically days off for every employee; check the applicable CAO or contract.
