# Lead with type, prove with photos

For a B2B HR / Employer-of-Record About page in 2025–2026, the strongest pattern is a **type-led hero**: a big mission headline, a short subhead and a one-line trust statement. Below it sits a **compact bento of real office/team photos with a separate, typographic 4-stat band**. Stat cards floating over photo corners and full-bleed stock heroes with overlaid numbers are not the current pattern. The peer evidence points this way, though not decisively. Of nine competitor and reference About pages fetched in October 2026, only Deel has a formal stat block: four numbers, each above its label, placed mid-page and never overlaid on a photo. Everyone else puts figures in prose or shows none ([Deel About](https://www.deel.com/about/); [Stripe](https://stripe.com/about); [Linear](https://linear.app/about)). Trend sources describe oversized type as having **"replaced the stock hero image as the default attention-grabber"** and call bento grids "mainstream" ([Line25](https://line25.com/articles/web-design-trends-2026/)). Usability research says real photos of people and places get attention while decorative imagery is ignored ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/decorative-images/)). EOR peers mostly show leadership headshots, illustration or nothing, so a static black-and-white site with genuine office photography has a real chance to stand out. The concrete recommendation, detailed at the end, is a split hero: headline on the left, a three-photo mosaic on the right, and a full-width 4-up stat band underneath with hairline dividers. Stats use Inter tabular numerals and the warm orange appears only on the suffixes. There is one optional one-shot count-up, and every final value ships in the HTML.

## Competitors keep numbers out of photos and below the headline

The HR/EOR category is surprisingly consistent. Deel's /about opens with H1 "Work with the world", one photo and a trust line ("Trusted by 40,000+ companies from startups to enterprise"), followed by a looping customer-logo strip. Its **four-stat grid** (40,000 businesses, $17.3B payments, 650,000+ workers hired, 150 countries) comes only after the origin story and timeline, as proof that backs up the narrative ([Deel About](https://www.deel.com/about/)). Rippling pairs its mission H1 with a platform illustration and has **no stat cards**: "600+ apps" appears only in body copy, and its 10 offices are listed as text with no photos ([Rippling About](https://www.rippling.com/about)). Remote shows **no numbers at all**, just values and a 9-person leadership grid ([Remote About](https://remote.com/about)). Oyster's only stat block, a 3-up "2020 / Year founded, 648+ / Employees, 70 / Countries", lives on its Careers page ([Oyster Careers](https://www.oysterhr.com/careers)).

General SaaS leans even more editorial. Stripe weaves scale into prose ("more than 5 million businesses", "around 1.7% of global GDP") ([Stripe](https://stripe.com/about)). Linear uses a hero video and an inline "more than 40,000 companies" ([Linear](https://linear.app/about)). Vercel and Notion show no stats ([Vercel](https://vercel.com/about); [Notion](https://www.notion.com/about)). A wider roundup of 16 B2B About pages confirms the pattern: stats are an early headline number (Calendly's "20+ million") or a cleanly separated section (Airtable, Contentful, Miro). The photo-led heroes (Surfe, Clay, ClickUp) use large team photos with no numbers on top ([Webstacks](https://www.webstacks.com/blog/about-us-page)).

Two details matter for a smaller EOR brand. First, **leaders let figures drift**. Deel's About page says both 35,000+ and 40,000 customers and both 150 and 100 countries. Oyster claims 60+ countries on one page and 70 on another. None of the fetched pages dates or footnotes a stat ([Deel About](https://www.deel.com/about/); [Oyster Who We Are](https://www.oysterhr.com/who-we-are)). A single-sourced, dated set ("as of Q3 2026") is cheap credibility. Second, **real office photography is rare** among remote-first EOR peers, who rely on headshots, framed composites or illustration. Real photos are therefore a differentiator, not a cliché.

A caveat: these observations come from a text fetcher. Rendered type sizes, dividers and whether Deel animates its counters were not verified, and Papaya Global's page blocked access.

## Six layouts compete, and two fit a 3–4 photo, 4-stat hero

| Pattern | 2025–26 status | Best fit | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Type-led hero + full-width stat band | Most common on SaaS About pages ([Webstacks](https://www.webstacks.com/blog/about-us-page)) | Numbers are the proof; 0–1 photo | Can feel generic without imagery |
| Split hero + photo mosaic + stat band | Current; the "split layout" alternative to full-bleed heroes ([Peerlist](https://peerlist.io/saxenashikhil/articles/hero-images-are-dead-these-solutions-are-replacing-them)) | 2–4 real photos, 3–4 stats | Lowest risk; photos never compete with numbers |
| Bento grid (photo + stat tiles) | "Mainstream" ([Line25](https://line25.com/articles/web-design-trends-2026/)); used by Vercel, WorkOS, Mercury, Attio ([SaaSFrame](https://www.saasframe.io/patterns/bento-grid)) | 6–9 tiles of fixed-length content | Flat checkerboard if tiles are uniform |
| Editorial asymmetric collage | Rising in 2026 ([B12](https://www.b12.io/resource-center/website-design/website-layout-ideas-to-try-in-2026.html)) | Human, "non-template" feel | Harder to make responsive |
| Photo marquee | Logo marquees common; photo marquees niche | 6+ equal photos | Needs a pause control under WCAG 2.2.2 |
| Stat card overlapping a photo corner | Restrained layering survives; glass badges date to the ~2021 wave ([Line25](https://line25.com/articles/web-design-trends-2026/)) | 1 photo, 1–2 stats | Covers faces, breaks on mobile, reads as template |

No study measures how common these patterns are. The widely quoted bento adoption figures (45% from Line25, 67% from B12) conflict and have no published method, so treat them as marketing ([B12](https://www.b12.io/resource-center/website-design/web-design-guide-bento-grids-and-kinetic-typography/)). The bento rules that do have support are structural. Use **4- or 6-column grids with fixed row heights**, for example four columns × 200px rows with a 14px gap. **Six to nine tiles is the sweet spot**, and grids break down past about a dozen. **Tile size must reflect real importance**. Content must be fixed-length, because fixed rows clip long tiles ([The Plus Addons](https://theplusaddons.com/blog/bento-grid-layout/); [freeCodeCamp](https://www.freecodecamp.org/news/bento-grids-in-web-design/)). That makes a full bento risky for an About hero with only three or four photos. Too few tiles leaves it sparse, and padding it with stat tiles produces the uniform checkerboard both tutorials warn against.

On mobile, bento and split layouts collapse by remapping `grid-template-areas`. A four-column grid becomes two columns at about 767px and the focal tile stays full width ([The Plus Addons](https://theplusaddons.com/blog/bento-grid-layout/)). Visual reordering does not change screen-reader or keyboard order, so the HTML must already be in reading order ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/docs/Web/CSS/Guides/Grid_layout/Common_grid_layouts)). A 4-up stat band becomes a clean 2×2, which is the most robust collapse of any pattern here.

## Big tabular numerals, tiny tracked labels, one accent

No authoritative source fixes a number-to-label size ratio. Practice converges on a large numeral and a much smaller label, roughly **3.5:1 to 6:1**. In Inter, that means a value at `clamp(2.75rem, 2rem + 3.5vw, 5rem)` (about 44–80px), weight 600, line-height 1. The label sits at 13–14px, weight 500, uppercase and capped near 18ch. Inter's tracking formula settles at **about -0.022em at display sizes** ([Inter dynamic metrics](https://d.rsms.me/inter-website/v3/dynmetrics/)). All-caps labels need **0.05–0.12em of added letterspacing** ([Practical Typography](https://practicaltypography.com/letterspacing.html)). Set the numerals with `font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums lining-nums`, which has been Baseline since 2020 ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/font-variant-numeric)). Keep tabular figures out of the labels, because they "make ordinary words look uneven" ([Shopify](https://shopify.dev/docs/api/app-home/latest/web-components/typography-and-content/number)). Inter's `case` feature aligns "+" and similar symbols with digits ([Inter](https://rsms.me/inter/)). Shrinking the suffix to about 0.6em also stops a full-size "+" from dominating.

For an editorial black-and-white look, left-align the stats. Separate them with **1px vertical hairlines at about 15–20% contrast** and gaps of 2–4rem, and put 0.5–0.75rem between number and label. These are practitioner conventions, not cited standards. The orange accent belongs on **large text only**, such as the numeral suffix or the numerals themselves. Warm oranges usually fail 4.5:1 on white for small text but can pass the **3:1 threshold for large text** (about 24px or larger) ([W3C SC 1.4.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html)). Check the actual hex before shipping: the current CSS has its "orange" tokens set to #111111, so the accent value is not yet settled. Grey labels need care too: #6b6b6b on white passes at about 5.3:1, and #999 fails at about 2.8:1.

## Motion should be one-shot, brief, and invisible to screen readers

NN/g says animation "must be unobtrusive, brief, and subtle", with most durations **between 100 and 500ms** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/animation-purpose-ux/); [NN/g duration](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/animation-duration/)). Parallax buys nothing: a controlled study found **no significant difference** in task time, errors or opinion with or without it ([OsloMet](https://oda.oslomet.no/oda-xmlui/handle/11250/2832354)). The tasteful set for a B2B hero is a single fade-and-rise of the stats (opacity plus a 12–24px translate, about 500ms, 60–100ms stagger), an optional one-time count-up, and hairlines that draw in with `transform: scaleX`. Avoid scroll-jacking, pinned stat sequences, counters that re-fire, bouncy easing and cursor effects.

Count-ups must be built defensively. Ship the **final value in the HTML**. Expose it to assistive technology through a visually hidden copy and set `aria-hidden="true"` on the animated span, the approach used by Framer's and WordPress's counter components ([Framer](https://www.framer.com/community/marketplace/components/better-counter/); [Counting Number Block](https://en-ca.wordpress.org/plugins/counting-number-block/)). Never use `aria-live`, which would announce every intermediate number. Never put `aria-label` on a bare div or span, because ARIA forbids naming the generic role ([WAI-ARIA 1.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/wai-aria-1.2/#generic)). Run the animation only when `prefers-reduced-motion: no-preference` matches and an IntersectionObserver fires, which aligns with WCAG technique C39 ([W3C SC 2.3.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/animation-from-interactions.html); [GSAP a11y](https://www.gsap.com/resources/a11y/)). A count under 5 seconds very likely falls outside WCAG 2.2.2's pause requirement. A looping photo marquee does not, and needs a pause control ([W3C SC 2.2.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/pause-stop-hide.html)). CSS scroll-driven animations (`animation-timeline: view()`) run off the main thread in Chrome 115+ and Safari 26+ ([Chrome for Developers](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/css-ui/scroll-driven-animations)). Firefox's default support is still disputed, so use them only as progressive enhancement ([caniuse](https://caniuse.com/mdn-css_properties_animation-timeline_view)).

Performance follows from the same principle: **never hide above-the-fold content until JavaScript runs.** The hero photo is the likely LCP element. Put it in the HTML as an `<img>` with `srcset`/`sizes`, explicit width and height, `fetchpriority="high"`, and **no lazy loading**. On Google Flights, `fetchpriority` alone cut LCP from 2.6s to 1.9s ([web.dev LCP](https://web.dev/articles/optimize-lcp); [web.dev Fetch Priority](https://web.dev/articles/fetch-priority)). Give secondary photos `loading="lazy"` and set an `aspect-ratio` on each container to hold CLS at or below 0.1 ([web.dev CLS](https://web.dev/articles/optimize-cls)). Do not animate a clip-path reveal on the LCP image, because it delays the paint that LCP measures. Self-host Inter as WOFF2 with a size-adjusted fallback so large numerals don't shift when the font swaps in.

## The design to build: split hero, three-photo mosaic, hairline stat band

This specification synthesizes the evidence above. It is not a cited template. It is sized for the three About photos already in the site (`site/assets/theme/about/office.jpg`, `team.jpg`, `globe.jpg`) and leaves room for a fourth.

**Structure (desktop, 12-column container, max ~1200px).** The left five columns hold an uppercase eyebrow ("About us"), an H1 mission statement at about 56–72px (weight 600, -0.022em), a two-line subhead in grey #555, and a single trust line ("Trusted by N companies across M countries"). The right seven columns hold a **mosaic of three photos with mixed ratios**: `team.jpg` is a tall tile spanning two rows (the focal and LCP image, `fetchpriority="high"`), and `office.jpg` and `globe.jpg` stack beside it. Use a 12px gap and one shared radius of 0–8px for an editorial feel rather than app-card rounding. Grade all photos as a set: either all pre-processed grayscale or all warm-neutral. Pre-processed files beat a CSS `filter`, and the orange then stands out as the only colour. A fourth photo, if one arrives, turns the mosaic into a 2×2 with one tall tile, not a uniform checkerboard. **Below, across the full width**, sits a 1px top rule and a **4-up stat band**, left-aligned, separated by 1px vertical hairlines at about 15% black. Each stat has a numeral (clamp 44–80px, weight 600, tabular lining figures) with the suffix ("+", "%") at 0.6em in the warm orange, and an uppercase 13px label at 0.08em tracking in #555. A small "Figures as of [month year]" footnote closes the band. The four stats should be the category-standard set, all drawn from one source of truth: clients, countries covered, workers employed/onboarded, and years in operation or payroll processed.

**Markup and behaviour.** Use `<dl>` with label-then-value source order and flip it visually with flex `column-reverse`. Each value carries a visually hidden final number plus an `aria-hidden` animated span that already contains the real final number. Lazy-load the two secondary photos with explicit dimensions. Write descriptive alt text for team and office photos and use `alt=""` for the purely atmospheric globe image. One small vanilla-JS module, gated on reduced motion and an IntersectionObserver at about 0.5 threshold, counts each stat once, from 60–70% of its value to the final value, over about 1.5s with an ease-out curve. It formats with `Intl.NumberFormat` and staggers the stats by 80ms. Hairlines can draw in with `scaleX`. Nothing else moves. The headline and LCP photo render immediately, with no marquee and no parallax.

**Responsive.** On tablet, the mosaic drops below the text at full width with `team.jpg` at 16:9, and the stat band becomes a 2×2 with cross-shaped hairlines. On mobile (<640px) the order is headline, then the focal photo, then the 2×2 stats, then the two secondary photos as a 2-up row. The band goes to a single column only below about 360px.

## Conclusion

The evidence reframes the question. The best About heroes in this category do not fuse photos and stats into one clever object. They separate the jobs: type carries the mission, real photography carries the trust, and a quiet, dated stat band carries the proof. For a black-and-white EOR brand, two cheap moves set it apart from bigger peers: showing a genuine office and team (Deel, Remote and Oyster mostly can't or don't) and publishing a consistent, dated set of numbers, which the leaders fail to do across their own pages.

Some things remain unknown. No study ranks these layouts by conversion or comprehension. Visual details of competitor stat blocks need screenshot verification. The accent hex must pass a contrast check before it touches anything smaller than about 24px. The recommended design is low-risk precisely because it does not depend on any of those answers.
